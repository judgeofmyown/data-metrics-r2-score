np.random.seed(42)
def generate_1d_data(l: int):
    x = np.random.rand(l)*10
    y = 2*x + 4*(x**2) + [np.random.rand(1).tolist()[0] for i in range(l)]
    return x, y
x, y = generate_1d_data(5)
idx = np.argsort(x)
output_avg = y.mean()
plt.plot(x[idx], np.full(len(x), output_avg), marker='x', label='mean')
plt.plot(x[idx], y[idx], marker='o')
plt.vlines(
    x[idx], 
    [min(output_avg, y[i]) for i in idx],
    [max(output_avg, y[i]) for i in idx],
    colors='red',
    label='residuals'
)

plt.xlabel("inputs")
plt.ylabel("Outputs")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

residuals_mean = [(y[i] - output_avg) for i in idx]
residuals_variance = sum([r**2 for r in residuals_mean]) / len(residuals_mean)
residuals_variance.round(3)

<h2 id="The-variance-of-the-regression-line-model-computed-as">The variance of the regression line model computed as¶</h2><p>$$ \begin{aligned} Var_{pred} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{1}^{n}(y_{pred} - y_i)^2 \\ \implies Var_{pred} &= 858.148 \end{aligned} $$</p>
<h3 id="Therefore-we-can-have-a-metric-of-how-much-variance-is-explained-by-the-line-regression-model">Therefore we can have a metric of how much variance is explained by the line regression model¶</h3><h3 id="That-will-be-the-fraction-of-variance-reduced">That will be the fraction of variance reduced¶</h3><p>$$ \begin{aligned} metric &= \frac{Var_{mean} - Var_{pred}}{Var_{mean}} \times 100 \\ &= \frac{16777.555 - 858.148}{16777.555} \times 100 \\ &= 94.885\% \\ &= 0.948 \end{aligned} $$</p> <h4 id="This-infact-is-the-$%7BR%5E2-score%7D$-metric,-and-this-implies-the-fraction-of-variance-explained-by-the-model-compared-to-the-residual-variance-from-using-just-the-mean.">This infact is the ${R^2 score}$ metric, and this implies the fraction of variance explained by the model compared to the residual variance from using just the mean.¶</h4>
<h2 id="Drawback-to-$%7BR%5E2-score%7D$">Drawback to ${R^2 score}$¶</h2><p>The standard $R^2$ is gullible metric. It blindly rewards for adding more variables to the model, even if the new variable data is garbage.Because of this, it is a dangerous measure of a model's true predictive power if used in isolation.</p> <p>When we train a regression model the underlying algorithm's objective is to minimize the error (computed by for e.g. least squares) in our training dataset. Now lets imagine we are working on a dataset and we added a completely irrelevant/useless variable to the dataset, the algorithm will find some microscopic, accidental pattern linking to that irrelevant variable to the target in our specific dataset.</p> <p>Because the algorithm found that tiny accidental correlation, it assigns a small weight to that irrelevant variable.This slightly improves the model's fit on the training data, shrinking the total error.</p>
  • if the random variable helps (even accidently), the total error goes down
  • If the random variable is mathematically perfectly useless (incredibly rare in real data), the algorithm will assign it a weight of zero, and the total error stays exactly the same.
<p>Because $R^2$ is calculated based on how much the error is reduced, standard $R^2$ can only increase of stay flat. It can never decrease when a variable is added.</p> <p>This flaw leads directly to overfitting. One might just throw dozens of variables into a model to chase a $0.99 R^2$ score. The model looks perfect on paper because it learnt the noise in the training data, but it will fail entirely when predicting real-world, unseen data.</p>
<h3 id="Fix:-Adjusted-$R%5E2$">Fix: Adjusted $R^2$¶</h3><p>The formula is as: $$ \text{Adj. } R^2 = 1 - \frac{(1-R^2)(n-1)}{(n-k-1)} $$ Where: $$ \begin{aligned} n &= \text{sample size} \\ k &= \text{number of independent predictors} \\ R^2 &= \text{standard R-squared} \end{aligned} $$</p>
<p>When we add a predictor that genuinely improves the model, Adjusted $R^2$ increases. When you add a predictor that contributes little, Adjusted $R^2$ decreases or stays flat. This makes it the correct metric for comparing multiple linear regressions models with different numbers of predictors.</p>
